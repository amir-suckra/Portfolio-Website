Introduction to Aerospace Engineering

Airfoil Design

Aerospace CAD Airfoil

Project Goal

Goal: understand how an airfoil's shape and angle of attack create lift and drag by analyzing a real one. I chose the NACA 23018 airfoil used on the Grumman F8F Bearcat, predicted its performance, and turned it into a 3D wing section I could print.

What I Built

I used JavaFoil to calculate lift, drag, and moment coefficients from 0° to 30° angle of attack, then built a spreadsheet that predicts lift and drag forces at airspeeds from 10 to 60 mph. I scaled the airfoil coordinates to a 3.5-inch chord, imported them into Autodesk Inventor, and modeled the wing section for 3D printing.

Build Process

  1. Choose an aircraft. Researched World War II Navy fighters and their wing sections, then chose the NACA 23018 profile from the Grumman F8F Bearcat.
  2. Analyze the airfoil. Ran the profile through JavaFoil to get lift, drag, and moment coefficients from 0° to 30° angle of attack.
  3. Predict real forces. Built a spreadsheet that turns those coefficients into lift and drag forces at airspeeds from 10 to 60 mph for my wing area.
  4. Scale the geometry. Scaled the normalized airfoil coordinates to a 3.5-inch chord.
  5. Model it in CAD. Imported the coordinates into Autodesk Inventor, extruded the wing section, and exported it as an STL for 3D printing.

My Role

This was an individual project. I chose the airfoil, ran the JavaFoil analysis, built the lift and drag spreadsheet, and modeled the wing section in Inventor.

Key Problem Solved

Turning a published airfoil profile into a real, buildable part: getting from normalized coordinates and dimensionless coefficients to a scaled CAD model and force predictions for an actual wing.

What I Learned

How angle of attack drives lift, drag, and stall; reading airfoil performance data; using a spreadsheet as an engineering model; and building precise CAD geometry from coordinate data.
